Hamiltonians of a problem ​

The indirect method works with the Hamiltonians of the maximum principle. They depend only on the problem, so they need not be written by hand: OptimalControl builds them from the model, with the conventions of Notation and conventions.

ObjectBy handFrom the problem
pseudo-Hamiltonian    H(x, p, u) = …pseudo_hamiltonian(ocp)
Hamiltonian of a control law,  h(x, p) = …hamiltonian(ocp, u)
Hamiltonian of a control-free problemh(x, p) = …hamiltonian(ocp)
the same, along an arc on which constraints are activeconstraints= and multipliers= keywords

They are also read back from a flow, see Accessors. This page shows how to get them, how to call them, and what to do with them: build a flow, write a shooting function, or check the conditions of the maximum principle.

julia
using OptimalControl        # problems, Hamiltonians, flows
using OrdinaryDiffEqTsit5   # integrator of the flows
using ForwardDiff           # derivatives, to check the maximum principle

The pseudo-Hamiltonian ​

The problem of this page is the energy minimisation of the double integrator:

julia
t0, tf = 0, 1
x0, xf = [-1, 0], [0, 0]

ocp = @def begin
    t ∈ [t0, tf], time
    x = (q, v) ∈ R², state
    u ∈ R, control
    x(t0) == x0
    x(tf) == xf
    ẋ(t) == [v(t), u(t)]
    0.5∫(u(t)^2) → min
end

Its pseudo-Hamiltonian, in the normal case   , is

pseudo_hamiltonian builds it from the model:

julia
H = pseudo_hamiltonian(ocp)
PseudoHamiltonian: autonomous, fixed (no variable)
  natural call: h̃(x, p, u)
  uniform call: h̃(t, x, p, u, v)

It is called with its natural signature, which follows the problem: here, an autonomous problem without variable, H(x, p, u):

julia
x, p, u = [1.0, 2.0], [3.0, 4.0], 0.5
H(x, p, u)      # p₁v + p₂u − u²/2 = 6 + 2 − 0.125
7.875

The uniform signature, H(t, x, p, u, v), takes every argument whatever the problem, with nothing for a missing variable:

julia
H(0.0, x, p, u, nothing)
7.875
ProblemNatural call
autonomous, no variableH(x, p, u)
autonomous, with a variableH(x, p, u, v)
non-autonomous, no variableH(t, x, p, u)
non-autonomous, with a variableH(t, x, p, u, v)

As everywhere in OptimalControl, a one-dimensional state, control or variable is a number.

The Hamiltonian of a control law ​

The maximisation of in gives the control in feedback form,  . The Hamiltonian of this law is

and hamiltonian(ocp, u) builds it:

julia
u_max(x, p) = p[2]
h = hamiltonian(ocp, u_max)
h(x, p)         # p₁v + p₂²/2 = 6 + 8
14.0

The law is a plain function with the natural arity of the problem (u(x, p), u(t, x, p), u(x, p, v) or u(t, x, p, v)), or a typed law, see Passing a typed law. The keyword p0 applies as for the pseudo-Hamiltonian.

A control-free problem has a Hamiltonian and no pseudo-Hamiltonian: hamiltonian(ocp), without a law, builds it.

julia
ocp_cf = @def begin
    t ∈ [0, 1], time
    x ∈ R, state
    x(0) == 1
    ẋ(t) == -x(t)
    ∫(x(t)^2) → min
end
hamiltonian(ocp_cf)(2.0, 3.0)   # p·(−x) − x² = −6 − 4
-10.0

What to do with them ​

Build a flow ​

The flow of a problem and its law, Flow(ocp, u), is the flow of the Hamiltonian of the law. Building the flow from the Hamiltonian, as in From Hamiltonians, gives the same extremal:

julia
p0 = [12.0, 6.0]
f = Flow(ocp, u_max)
g = Flow(h)
f(t0, x0, p0, tf), g(t0, x0, p0, tf)
(([-4.158277980903794e-16, 5.038973481527467e-15], [12.0, -5.999999999999998]), ([-4.158277980903794e-16, 5.038973481527467e-15], [12.0, -5.999999999999998]))

Conversely, a flow gives back the Hamiltonian it integrates, the very same object:

julia
hamiltonian(f) === h
true

Write a shooting function ​

A shooting function often needs a Hamiltonian, for instance for the transversality condition of a free final time. It is safer to get it from the problem than to write it again: the formula is written once, in the model. See Shooting, where the condition    uses the pseudo-Hamiltonian of the problem.

The maximisation condition says that   along the optimal law, when the control is in the interior of the control set. With ForwardDiff:

julia
∂uH(x, p, u) = ForwardDiff.derivative(w -> H(x, p, w), u)
∂uH(x, p, u_max(x, p))

Partial and total derivatives ​

The flow of a law can be built in two ways (keyword hamiltonian_type of Flow, see Total or partial Hamiltonian):

  • :total differentiates  , through the law:

  • :partial takes the partial derivatives of , with the control frozen at the value of the law, at  , as in the maximum principle.

The two differ by the terms in . For the optimal law, this term vanishes, so the two coincide:

julia
∇p_total = ForwardDiff.gradient(q -> h(x, q), p)
∇p_partial = ForwardDiff.gradient(q -> H(x, q, u_max(x, p)), p)
∇p_total, ∇p_partial
([2.0, 4.0], [2.0, 4.0])

For a law that does not maximise , such as   , it does not:   , and the two gradients differ. The second component of is : only the partial derivative gives     , the dynamics that the law actually drives.

julia
law(x, p) = p[2] + 1
h_law = hamiltonian(ocp, law)
∂uH(x, p, law(x, p)), ForwardDiff.gradient(q -> h_law(x, q), p), ForwardDiff.gradient(q -> H(x, q, law(x, p)), p)
(-1.0, [2.0, 4.0], [2.0, 5.0])

The same holds for the multipliers of the constraints, below: the total derivative goes through the multiplier in feedback form, the partial one freezes it. Both coincide on an optimal arc, where the constraint is active.

Constraints ​

Constraints by label ​

On an arc where constraints are active, the maximum principle adjoins them to the pseudo-Hamiltonian, with their multipliers . They are given by their label, and the multiplier is an explicit argument, like the control:

where   is the constraint on its active side. The example is the speed limit: the velocity of the double integrator is bounded by  .

julia
v_max = 1.2
ocp_c = @def begin
    t ∈ [t0, tf], time
    x = (q, v) ∈ R², state
    u ∈ R, control
    v(t) ≤ v_max, (v_con)
    x(t0) == x0, (initial)
    x(tf) == xf
    ẋ(t) == [v(t), u(t)]
    0.5∫(u(t)^2) → min
end

Hc = pseudo_hamiltonian(ocp_c; constraints=:v_con)
μ = 2.0
Hc(x, p, u, μ) - H(x, p, u)     # μ (v_max − v) = 2 (1.2 − 2)
-1.5999999999999996

Several labels are given as a tuple, constraints=(:a, :b): then stacks their multipliers, in that order. The labels not given are inactive.

A control box is inert in the flow: on an arc where the control is saturated,  , the term    vanishes, and so do its derivatives in and . Its multiplier is given by the stationarity   . This is why it is usually left out; adjoining it gives its multiplier, to compare with the dual of a direct method.

With a control law, the multipliers of the active constraints are given in feedback form, as label => μ pairs, and the Hamiltonian of the arc is

On the boundary arc of the speed limit,  , so  ; the maximisation condition gives  , and the adjoint equation      gives  :

The flow of the arc is Flow(ocp_c, (x, p) -> 0; constraint=:v_con, multiplier=μ_b), see Constrained arcs. The speed limit example solves the problem by shooting, and compares the multiplier with the dual of a direct method.
